## Pitch

Keep this section to about 1,000 words.

### Concept

What it is, in a few sentences.

*Draft for Developer review; wording and scope are the Developer's to change.*

A game that helps pre-dental students, dental students, and dental assistants get a feel for the flow of common dental procedures. The procedures are kept general, such as crown prep, fillings, root canals, flossing, polishing, tray setup, and sterilization. The game states clearly that it is a study aid and that steps vary by dentist, office, and state.

Flow (Developer's description): the player opens the game, presses Start, and picks one of six modes:
1. Floss and polish
2. Composite filling
3. Crown prep
4. Crown seat
5. Root canal
6. Sterilization sort: decide which items go in cold sterile and which do not (for example, handpieces do not).

Modes 1 to 5 are playable procedures in first person. The player sees the scene from the assistant's point of view: a dentist's arm in the frame, a patient with their mouth open, and an instrument and materials tray in the corner of the screen. At each step the dentist cues the player by holding out a hand, and the player hands over the correct instrument or material. Each step has a 10-second countdown and earns one point per whole second left when the correct item is handed over, and the session ends with a score out of 100. Some steps include a wait, such as holding for the cure time. Short popups explain where a step can vary by dentist. This replaces the earlier idea of dragging step cards into order, which the Developer found too plain, and it fits the Developer's own difficulty when starting as an RDA: learning the steps of each procedure.

Mode 6 is a short sorting minigame and is unchanged. Sterilization items and answers must be checked against a published source and a reviewer, not memory. Items should be generic ("ultrasonic scaler", not a brand name).

Decisions made by the Developer:
- Build composite filling first, as the main test of the approach. The Developer leans toward actual assisting procedures over floss and polish, which may be built last or cut because it is generic.
- Feedback follows a single general outline for each procedure. Differences between dentists are shown as popups and in the disclaimer, not graded.
- First-person view, dentist cues each step by holding out a hand, tray in a corner.
- Scoring is out of 100 at the end of each session, with 10 steps worth up to 10 points each. Each step has a 10-second countdown. Handing over the correct item with 10 seconds left earns 10 points, with 9 left earns 9, and so on. If the countdown runs out, the step earns 0. Example: steps scoring 9, 10, 5, 10, 3, 6, 7, 8, 10, 6 give a score of 74. The tray stays on screen the whole time, and the Developer's rationale is that the pressure is similar to real chairside work.
- The sorting mode stays as a fun minigame.
- A wrong pick gives feedback that directs the player to the correct next step.
- A tutorial mode and/or an in-game guide listing each procedure's steps in order, so players can read and memorize them.
- Timing matters because dentists appreciate speed: the player should not need more than 10 seconds to select the correct next step. The cure wait is separate and is not timed against the player.
- Art assets will be generated with AI to save time (see Risks for disclosure and checks).
- The Developer plans to use JavaScript.

Open decisions for the Developer:
- Order of the remaining modes, and which are cut if time runs short.
- What happens when the countdown runs out. Proposal: the step scores 0 and a hint highlights the correct item, so a stuck beginner does not stall.
- Whether a perfect 100 should be reachable. Scoring 10 requires handing over the item the instant the cue appears, so strong players may top out around 70 to 80. Option: give full points for a handoff within the first second or two.
- How wrong picks count toward the score. Assumption: the countdown keeps running, so a wrong pick only costs time.
- What the score is called, since it measures both correct choices and speed.
- How many steps each procedure has, since steps are worth 100 divided by the step count.
- Whether the tutorial is an untimed play-through, a read-only guide, or both.
- Whether wrong picks cost points or only give feedback.
- Whether the timer can be turned off for players who need more time.
- Whether the sorting mode has a third bin (for example heat sterilization). Deferred.
- Logo, color, and type.

### Technical goals

What the system must do and the technical challenges you intend to take on.

*Draft for Developer review.*

- **Two reusable engines.** A procedure engine (a first-person scene where the dentist cues each step and the player hands over the correct item) and a sort engine (put items in bins). Each mode is content for one of them, so adding a procedure means adding data, not code.
- **Playable procedures.** The player acts out the assisting role, for example handing the dentist the bond for a filling, who takes it and places it; then the composite (flowable or packable); then the curing light, which makes the player wait the cure time (about 10 seconds) before the next step.
- **Session score out of 100.** Each step runs a 10-second countdown and records the time from cue to correct handoff. The step scores the whole seconds left (10 down to 0), and the session score is the sum, shown at the end of the session.
- **Variation popups.** Short popups tell the player where a step can vary by dentist, for example the filling material the dentist prefers, or a request for a tool to shape the composite at a given point. Cure time and material details need a published source and reviewer check.
- **A first-person scene** with a dentist's arm, a patient with mouth open, and an instrument tray in a corner, with simple animation for the handoff. Art is generated with AI tools, checked and edited by the Developer, disclosed as AI-generated, and never based on real patient images.
- **Tutorial mode and in-game guide** that show each procedure's steps in order, written in the Developer's own words.
- **Touch-friendly interaction** (tap or drag items from the tray) that works on phones and laptops, tested early.
- **Content in JSON data files,** separate from code, so a reviewer can read and correct each step (cue, correct item, accepted alternatives, popup text, wait time) directly.
- **Progress saved locally** in the browser, with no accounts and nothing sent to a server.
- **Public deployment** on GitHub Pages.

### Risks

What could block or derail the project, and how you will find out early.

*Draft for Developer review.*

Competitor and evidence claims below come from a small number of web searches and were not independently verified.

**Content accuracy and variation (highest risk).** Procedure steps vary by dentist, office, and state, and the Developer's memory of a year of work may differ from current practice. Planned handling: keep procedures general, show a visible study-aid disclaimer, use popups and accepted alternatives where practices differ, cite published sources, and ask reviewers (a pediatric dentist for pediatric content, and general dentists the Developer shadowed, if willing) to check the general flow. Reviewer availability is not yet confirmed. Find out early: ask a reviewer to check one procedure in Phase 2.

**Scope of practice.** What an RDA may do varies by state, so the game should not imply a standard of care. Find out early: decide which topics, if any, need a state caveat.

**Copyright.** Textbook text and figures are copyrighted. Plan: write in the Developer's own words and use AI-generated or licensed illustrations, not copied figures. The ADA's procedure codes (CDT) may be copyrighted; unverified, so do not use them without checking.

**Learning effectiveness.** Studies of dental serious games exist, with mixed or limited results (for example a crossover trial of 74 students on a histology game, and a study finding a game about as effective as paper for factual knowledge). The pitch makes no claim that this game improves learning. Find out early: a simple before-and-after test with a few users in Phase 3 or 4.

**Competition.** Related tools found: Dental Assisting Prep (tray setups and quizzes), Dentalverse (procedure protocols for students), Brainscape and Anki decks, and CDA exam prep apps. No free, game-style tool for the general flow of procedures was found, but only a few searches were run. Find out early: the Developer tries one or two of these.

**Content and art volume (raised by the first-person design).** Each procedure needs a cue per step, instrument and material icons, handoff animation, popups, and feedback, and the scene needs a dentist's arm, a patient's mouth, and a tray, all drawn or licensed by the Developer. Plan: use placeholder art for the Phase 2 MVP, build two or three procedures well before adding more, and cut modes before cutting quality. Find out early: time how long the composite filling takes to build in Phase 2 and use that to estimate the rest.

**Speed scoring and learning.** Time pressure can push learners to guess instead of think, and a bare outstretched hand gives a beginner no clue. Handling so far: wrong picks give feedback that points to the correct step, a tutorial and in-game guide let players learn the steps first, and a hint appears after the 10-second limit. The 10-second limit is a game rule, not a clinical standard, and the game says so. Find out early: watch a first-time player in Phase 2 and note where they stall.

**AI-generated art.** AI image tools often produce inconsistent characters and scenes, and can get dental instruments and anatomy wrong. Plan: generate a small reusable set (one scene background, a few dentist-arm poses, item icons) instead of a new image per step, have a reviewer check instruments, state that the art is AI-generated, and record in `research.md` the tool, its terms of use for the output, the prompts, and what the Developer edited. Tool terms on ownership and public use differ and have not been checked. Find out early: generate and test the composite filling set in Phase 2 before committing to the style.
