## Pitch

### Concept

Going Chairside is a browser game that helps pre-dental students, dental students, and dental assistants learn the flow of common dental procedures. The player sees the scene as the assistant: a dentist's arm, a patient with their mouth open, and an instrument tray in the corner. The dentist holds out a hand for each step, and the player hands over the right instrument or material before a 10-second countdown ends. Popups show where steps vary by dentist. From the start screen the player picks one of six modes: five procedures, beginning with composite filling, or a short sterilization minigame that sorts items into cold sterile or not. Procedures are kept general, and the game says it is a study aid.

### Title and branding

Working title: **Going Chairside**, with no subtitle. The start screen reads "a dental procedure study aid." The name does not say "RDA," so it fits all players. A search for "Chairside" found related dental products (such as the ADA Chairside Instructor) but no study game, and the title may change. Art is AI-generated and disclosed. First logo: a white tooth beside the name on lilac (#C8A2C8), in a clear sans-serif.

### Why this project

The Developer worked about a year as an RDA, shadowed general dentists, and had trouble learning the flow of procedures when starting out. It is worth five weeks because it solves a problem they lived, uses their own knowledge, needs no outside data set, and has a real interactive core to build and test.

### Users

Pre-dental students, dental students, and dental assistants who need to learn and remember the general order of common procedures. A few searches found study apps, flashcards, and exam prep, but no free game-style tool focused on procedure flow.

### Experience goals

Users should notice the dentist's hand, the tray, and the ticking countdown, and feel the pace of real chairside work with support from feedback and a tutorial. They should be able to recall a procedure's steps in order and hand over the right item quickly, and they should always know the game is a study aid, not clinical guidance.

### Technical goals

- **Two reusable engines:** a procedure scene and a sort game. Each step (cue, correct item, alternatives, popup, wait) is data, so a new procedure needs no new code.
- **Countdown scoring:** each step runs 10 seconds and scores the whole seconds left, with full 10 points for a handoff in the first 2 seconds. Ten steps give a score out of 100. A step that runs out scores 0.
- **Learning support:** a wrong pick gives feedback pointing to the correct step, a hint appears at zero, and a tutorial and in-game guide list the steps in order.
- **A first-person scene** with a reusable set of AI-generated images, and a cure wait that is not timed against the player.
- **Touch-friendly play** on phones and laptops.

### Starting point

From scratch. The repository began from the Project 5 template, whose commit comes first. Any library used will be named, license-checked, and credited in `research.md`.

### Tech stack

Plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript with no framework or backend, so every part can be explained simply. A canvas or layered images draw the scene, and input uses pointer events or a small library such as SortableJS (license to verify). Content is JSON, progress uses localStorage, and hosting is GitHub Pages.

### MVP or critical function

**Composite filling, end to end:** a start screen, a six-mode menu (other modes read "coming soon"), and the first-person procedure with cues, handoffs, the cure wait, popups, the countdown score, and the disclaimer. Placeholder art is acceptable if the scene and timing work.

It works when:
- Scoring matches a written test list (the run 9, 10, 5, 10, 3, 6, 7, 8, 10, 6 totals 74, plus a timeout and a wrong pick then a correct one), and the cure wait cannot be skipped.
- A first-time player finishes it on a phone and a laptop.
- A reviewer says the general outline is reasonable.

### Risks

Competitor and evidence claims come from a few web searches and are unverified.

- **Content accuracy and scope (highest risk).** Steps and RDA duties vary by dentist, office, and state, and the Developer's memory may differ from current practice. The game stays general, implies no standard of care, uses the Developer's own wording and published sources, and has a reviewer (unconfirmed) check one procedure in Phase 2.
- **AI-generated art.** AI tools can be inconsistent and get instruments wrong, so the plan is a small reusable image set, tested on the filling procedure in Phase 2 and checked by a reviewer. The art is disclosed as AI-generated, with the tool, terms, and prompts recorded in `research.md`.
- **Art and content volume.** Each procedure needs cues, icons, animation, and popups. Build two or three well, and cut modes before quality. Time the filling build in Phase 2 to estimate the rest.
- **Speed pressure.** Pressure can push learners to guess, and a bare outstretched hand gives beginners no clue. The tutorial, feedback, and hint address this, and the 10-second limit is a game rule, not a clinical standard. Watch a first-time player in Phase 2.
- **Learning evidence and competition.** Studies of dental games show mixed or limited results, so the pitch claims no learning gain and plans a before-and-after test. Related tools include Dental Assisting Prep and Dentalverse.
